OUR STORY

From Pakistan to Other Worlds

Scientific exploration begins with curiosity, and curiosity has no geographical boundaries.

Our landscapes. Our questions. Our place in planetary science.

MARS FOR ALL began with a simple question: could the landscapes around us help us explore the surfaces of other worlds?

From the rugged terrain of Kharan to the changing landscapes of Thar and Cholistan, Pakistan offers a remarkable starting point for asking scientific questions about planetary terrain.

By comparing these terrestrial landscapes with Jezero crater on Mars and Shackleton crater on the Moon, we aim to make planetary science more accessible, transparent and locally meaningful.

This project is also a reminder that participation in space science is not limited by where someone comes from. Students and researchers everywhere can contribute through curiosity, careful analysis and open scientific collaboration.

MARS FOR ALL is our invitation to look beyond familiar horizons while recognising the scientific possibilities within our own.

THE RESEARCH BEHIND THE EXPLORATION

Science Behind the Atlas

What can the landscapes of Earth teach us about the surfaces of other worlds? MARS FOR ALL investigates this question by comparing selected Pakistani landscapes with terrain from Mars and the Moon using harmonized elevation data and mathematical terrain analysis.

Our research question

How closely do selected landscapes in Pakistan resemble the terrain characteristics of selected regions on Mars and the Moon when compared using measurable topographic features?

01 · Earth

Kharan, Thar and Cholistan represent three selected Pakistani landscapes. Their elevation data provides the terrestrial side of our comparison.

02 · Mars

Jezero crater provides our Martian reference terrain, allowing us to investigate similarities between planetary and terrestrial landscapes.

03 · Moon

Shackleton crater provides our lunar reference, extending the comparison beyond Earth and Mars.

From elevation to terrain characteristics

Elevation data describes the height of a surface. From this information, terrain characteristics such as slope and roughness can be derived. Comparing their statistical distributions helps us investigate how different landscapes relate to one another.

Slope

Slope describes how steep the terrain is. It helps distinguish relatively level surfaces from areas with stronger elevation changes.

Roughness

Roughness describes local surface variation and helps characterize how uneven a terrain surface is.

Statistical comparison

Mean, median and 90th-percentile measurements summarize different aspects of slope and roughness.

Six terrain features = 3 slope statistics + 3 roughness statistics

These six measurements form the basis of our similarity framework. The comparison is a mathematical measure of selected terrain characteristics, not proof that two landscapes have identical geological histories.

RESEARCH AND METHODOLOGY

The Science Behind the Atlas

01 / RESEARCH QUESTION

Can Earth's landscapes help us understand other worlds?

This project investigates whether selected terrestrial landscapes in Pakistan share measurable topographic characteristics with selected lunar and Martian regions. The objective is preliminary terrain-analogue screening, not proof of environmental equivalence or landing-site safety.

02 / SITE SELECTION

Our study regions

Moon — Shackleton Crater: A lunar south-polar region represented by LOLA Site04 elevation data.

Mars — Jezero Crater: Our Martian reference region, represented by MOLA elevation data.

Earth: Kharan, Thar and Cholistan in Pakistan are our terrestrial candidate regions.

04 / MATHEMATICAL FRAMEWORK

Describing terrain mathematically

Elevation

z = z(x,y)

Elevation z is represented as a function of horizontal position x and y.

Slope

S = arctan(√((∂z/∂x)² + (∂z/∂y)²))

Slope describes the rate of elevation change across horizontal distance. The result can be expressed in degrees.

Local elevation variability

σ = √(Σ(zᵢ − z̄)² / n)

This standard-deviation definition describes elevation variability within a neighbourhood. We must verify the exact roughness calculation used in our software before claiming this is its implemented formula.

Difference between two measurements

Δ = |A − B|

A and B are corresponding terrain measurements. Δ is their absolute difference.

05 / DATA PROCESSING

Harmonizing terrain data

Our comparison workflow brings terrain datasets to approximately 463 m spatial resolution.

  • Inspect raster dimensions and metadata.
  • Check coordinate reference systems.
  • Identify valid and missing observations.
  • Harmonize spatial resolution.
  • Calculate terrain characteristics.

Harmonization creates a more consistent comparison scale but does not eliminate differences in data quality or uncertainty.

06 / COMPARISON LOGIC

Interpreting similarity

Our comparison examines selected terrain characteristics between reference and candidate regions.

The exact normalization, feature weights and similarity-score formula must be documented from the implemented algorithm. We will not invent a scoring equation or numerical result.

07 / RESULTS AND REPRODUCIBILITY

From data to evidence

The final report will include actual measurements, units, valid-data coverage, processing resolution and the calculation method for each result.

Candidate comparisons must be traceable to their source rasters and processing steps. No ranking should be presented without its supporting calculations.

08 / SCIENTIFIC LIMITATIONS

Terrain resemblance is not environmental equivalence

Earth does not reproduce the Moon's vacuum, polar illumination or reduced gravity, nor Mars' atmosphere and environmental conditions.

Topographic similarity alone cannot establish habitability, engineering suitability, resource availability or mission safety. Further geological, environmental and engineering investigation is necessary.

DATA QUALITY · UNCERTAINTY · VALIDATION

How Reliable Are Our Comparisons?

Scientific comparisons are only meaningful when their data quality, uncertainty and limitations are made transparent.

Measurement Uncertainty

Elevation measurements are not perfectly exact. The lunar dataset includes an elevation-uncertainty raster, allowing measurement uncertainty to be considered separately from terrain similarity.

Observation Coverage

The number and distribution of observations affect how confidently terrain can be characterized. Observation-count datasets are available for the Moon and Jezero crater. Coverage must not be confused with the percentage of valid pixels in the harmonized grid.

Resolution and Resampling

Datasets from different planetary bodies may originate at different spatial resolutions. Harmonization makes comparison possible, but resampling can affect local terrain details and derived measurements.

Missing Data

Areas without valid measurements must be distinguished from areas with measured terrain. Missing observations can affect the representativeness of regional statistics.

Weighting Sensitivity

The similarity model was tested under balanced, slope-focused and roughness-focused weighting scenarios. Kharan retained the highest ranking for both planetary reference regions across these tested scenarios.

Scientific Limitations

A high terrain-similarity score indicates resemblance in the selected measurements. It does not establish identical geological histories, composition, environmental conditions or suitability for a particular mission.

Transparency note: weighting sensitivity is not a statistical confidence interval. Measurement-error propagation, spatial block bootstrap, resolution sensitivity and independent validation remain separate analyses.

MATHEMATICS · COMPUTATIONAL MODEL

The Mathematics Behind the Atlas

Every comparison is built from measurable terrain properties. This section documents the equations used to transform elevation data into comparable scientific features.

1. Terrain Slope

Slope describes the steepness of the terrain, derived from the spatial gradients of elevation.

S = (180 / π) × arctan(√[(∂z/∂x)² + (∂z/∂y)²])

Here, z represents elevation, while ∂z/∂x and ∂z/∂y represent elevation gradients in the two horizontal directions. The result is expressed in degrees.

2. Local Terrain Roughness

Roughness measures local elevation variation using the standard deviation of valid elevation values in a 3 × 3 neighbourhood.

R = √[(1/n) × Σ(zᵢ − z̄)²]

Here, zᵢ represents each valid elevation value, z̄ is the local mean elevation, and n is the number of valid values in the neighbourhood. Roughness is expressed in metres.

3. Regional Statistical Features

For each region, slope and roughness are summarized using three statistics:

  • Mean: the arithmetic average.
  • Median: the middle value in the distribution.
  • P90: the 90th percentile, representing a high-end terrain value without relying only on the maximum.

These produce six comparison features: three slope statistics and three roughness statistics.

4. Feature-Level Similarity

Each corresponding Earth and planetary feature is compared using a ratio-based similarity score.

Sᵢ = 100 × min(|Aᵢ|, |Bᵢ|) / max(|Aᵢ|, |Bᵢ|)

Aᵢ and Bᵢ are the corresponding feature values from the two regions. If both values are zero, the feature similarity is defined as 100.

A score closer to 100 indicates closer numerical resemblance for that feature. This is a comparison of measured values, not a measure of geological identity.

5. Group Similarity

The three slope-feature scores are averaged to obtain slope similarity:

Slope Similarity = (S₁ + S₂ + S₃) / 3

The three roughness-feature scores are averaged separately:

Roughness Similarity = (S₄ + S₅ + S₆) / 3

6. Weighted Overall Similarity

The two group scores are combined using the selected slope and roughness weights.

Overall Similarity = (Slope Similarity × wₛ) + (Roughness Similarity × wᵣ)

The weights sum to 1. The model evaluates three scenarios:

  • Balanced: slope 0.50, roughness 0.50.
  • Slope emphasis: slope 0.70, roughness 0.30.
  • Roughness emphasis: slope 0.30, roughness 0.70.

The final score remains on a 0–100 scale. Rankings are ordered from highest to lowest overall similarity.

The website's comparison uses harmonized terrain metrics at approximately 463-metre resolution. These equations describe the comparison framework; the resulting scores remain dependent on source-data quality, spatial resolution and valid coverage.

EARTH · MOON · MARS

Two Worlds, One Comparative Framework

What happens when the same six-feature terrain comparison framework is applied to two very different planetary environments?

Jezero Crater — Mars

Kharan achieved a mean similarity of 66.411, compared with 15.1007 for Thar and 8.4114 for Cholistan.

Within the selected Earth regions, Kharan was the strongest numerical match to the Jezero reference across all three weighting scenarios.

Shackleton Crater — Moon

Kharan also ranked first against Shackleton, with a mean similarity of 13.0353. Thar scored 2.8375 and Cholistan scored 1.6194.

This result must be interpreted alongside the Moon's limited direct source-observation coverage of 9.1541% in the analysed Shackleton region.

What Remained Consistent?

Kharan ranked first among the three selected Pakistani landscapes for both planetary reference regions under all three tested weighting scenarios.

This is the most consistent comparative finding across the two investigations. It shows that the leading regional ranking persisted when the planetary reference changed.

What Was Different?

The numerical similarity values were substantially higher for the Jezero comparison than for the Shackleton comparison. This reflects the measured feature relationships within this particular model and dataset.

Scores from different planetary comparisons should not be interpreted as proof that one planetary landscape is geologically more similar to Earth in every respect. They describe resemblance across the six selected terrain statistics only.

Why Data Coverage Matters

For Jezero, 100% of valid source pixels were identified as observed. However, valid coverage across the harmonized target grid was approximately 32.4524%.

These percentages describe different stages and different denominators. They must not be confused with each other or directly compared with Shackleton's 9.1541% direct source-observation coverage.

Across both planetary comparisons, Kharan retained the highest ranking in the tested scenarios. This is a robust ranking within the tested weighting choices, not proof of universal geological similarity or statistical certainty.

THE LUNAR INVESTIGATION · SHACKLETON CRATER

Looking Beyond Earth: The Lunar Finding

Our comparison with the Moon produced a consistent ranking, but it also revealed why planetary data coverage matters when interpreting scientific results.

Kharan: The Leading Lunar Terrain Match

Among Kharan, Thar and Cholistan, Kharan produced the highest mean similarity to the Shackleton crater reference in our six-feature terrain model.

Kharan: 13.0353

Thar scored 2.8375 and Cholistan scored 1.6194. Kharan remained the highest-ranked region across all three tested weighting scenarios.

A Critical Discovery About Lunar Data Coverage

The Shackleton source dataset had approximately 9.1541% direct source-observation coverage in the analysed region. This describes the proportion of source pixels identified as directly observed, not the percentage of valid pixels in the harmonized comparison grid.

These are different measures and must not be treated as interchangeable. A harmonized grid can contain valid values even where direct source observations are limited.

Why This Changes How We Interpret the Result

Limited or uneven observation coverage can affect how representative the measured terrain statistics are. Resampling and harmonization also do not create new independent observations.

The lunar similarity score should therefore be read as a comparison of the available processed terrain measurements, with source coverage kept visible as an important limitation.

What We Can and Cannot Conclude

We can report that Kharan ranked first among the three selected Pakistani regions under the tested weighting scenarios.

We cannot conclude from this score alone that Kharan is geologically identical to Shackleton, that the lunar surface is fully represented by the available observations, or that the comparison establishes a mission-ready analogue.

Lunar interpretation must be considered alongside the LOLA elevation-uncertainty and observation-count products. The existence of these datasets does not itself constitute a completed uncertainty-propagation analysis.

TRANSPARENCY · REPRODUCIBILITY

Research Integrity & Reproducibility

The Analog Atlas distinguishes original observations, derived terrain measurements and model-based interpretations.

Original Observations

Elevation and observation products originate from established planetary and Earth-observation datasets. Their source institutions and product references are documented in this Atlas.

Derived Measurements

Slope, roughness and regional statistical summaries are computationally derived from elevation data. They are not direct measurements of geological composition or environmental conditions.

Model-Based Findings

Similarity scores are outputs of a six-feature comparison framework. Rankings depend on the selected regions, features, data preparation and weighting assumptions.

Reproducibility

The project separates source datasets, processed terrain products, analysis scripts and website visualizations to make the research workflow easier to trace and review.

Uncertainty Still Under Investigation

Measurement-error propagation, spatial block bootstrap, confidence intervals, resolution sensitivity, missing-data sensitivity and independent validation remain separate scientific tasks.

Responsible Conclusions

The Atlas identifies numerical terrain resemblance within a defined model. It does not claim geological equivalence, identical planetary histories or mission suitability.
